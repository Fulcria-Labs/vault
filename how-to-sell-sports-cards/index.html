How to Sell Sports Cards (Where to Sell, Fees, and What You'll Actually Net)

The right venue for each card, the fees that quietly eat your profit, how to price from real comps, and how to ship so the card arrives in the condition you listed.

Selling a card well is a different skill from buying one. The same card can net you wildly different money depending on where you list it, how you priced it, whether you graded it first, and whether it survives the mail. This guide walks the whole sale — venue, price, grade-or-not, shipping, and the net-proceeds math — so you keep more of what the card is worth.

1. Pick the right place to sell

There is no single best marketplace — there's a best one for this card, at this price, on your timeline. Here's how the main options actually compare:

VenueTypical feesBest for
eBay~13% + $0.30/orderSingles over ~$20; deepest buyer pool and best comp history.
COMCListing + commission + cash-out feeHigh-volume lower-value cards you don't want to ship one at a time.
Whatnot / live breaks~8–12% seller feeFast sales to a live audience; boxes, lots and hype players.
Facebook groups / Instagram~3% (payment) or cashEstablished communities; lower fees but you handle trust and shipping.
Local card show / shop0% (or a buy-price haircut)Instant cash, no shipping — but dealers pay wholesale, not retail.

Rule of thumb: the more convenient and instant the sale, the more margin you give up. A dealer at a show paying cash today will offer well below the eBay sold price, because they need their own margin. That's a fair trade when you want the money now or the card is low-value; it's an expensive one on a card that would move itself online.

2. Price it from real comps — not the asking prices

The number that matters is what cards actually sold for, not what hopeful sellers are asking. Before you list:

  • Filter eBay to Sold listings for the exact card — same set, parallel, card number and grade.
  • Cross-check on a comps tool (130point, market-price apps) and look at the last 30–90 days, not a single lucky sale.
  • For graded cards, match the exact grade and grader — a PSA 9 and a raw "mint" copy are different markets.

Then decide between a fixed Buy It Now near recent sold prices (best for confident pricing) and an auction (best for hot, scarce, or hard-to-comp cards where you want the market to set the price).

3. Grade first, or sell it raw?

Grading is a bet you make before the sale, and it only makes sense when the graded sale price beats the raw price by more than the cost and wait of grading. Compare three numbers: the raw sold price today, the likely graded sold price, and the all-in grading cost.

If a raw card sells for $30 and a PSA 10 sells for $220 — but the same card at PSA 8 sells for $35 — grading is a coin-flip on centering and edges, not a sure thing. Cheap and common cards almost never justify the fee; high-end cards with a big PSA-10 premium usually do. Not sure who to send to? 4. Know your net before you list

The mistake that makes flips feel more profitable than they are: counting the sale price as profit. Your real proceeds look more like this:

LineExample
Sale price$120.00
Marketplace + payment fees (~13.25%)−$15.90
Shipping paid by you (if free shipping)−$5.00
Supplies (sleeve, top-loader, mailer)−$1.50
Grading cost (if you slabbed it)−$18.00
Original purchase price−$45.00
Actual net proceeds$34.60

Do this math before you set a price, not after the payout lands. It tells you your true floor — the price below which you're losing money — and over dozens of sales it reveals which players, sets and price tiers actually make you money.

5. Ship so the card survives

A card that arrives damaged becomes a return, a refund, and a negative rating. Protect the grade you sold:

  • Raw cards: penny sleeve → top-loader or card saver → tape the top-loader closed (not the card) → between two pieces of cardboard in a bubble mailer or plain white envelope (PWE) with tracking for lower-value cards.
  • Graded slabs: bubble-wrap the slab, brace it so it can't slide, and ship in a rigid box — slabs crack in flimsy mailers.
  • Anything valuable: add tracking and, above your comfort threshold, signature confirmation and insurance. Photograph the card and the packed parcel before it goes out.

FAQ

Where is the best place to sell sports cards?

For most singles over about $20, eBay still has the deepest buyer pool and the most reliable comps. High-volume, lower-value cards do better on COMC or in bulk lots; live audiences suit Whatnot; and local card shows or Facebook groups let you avoid marketplace fees if you can meet a buyer. Match the venue to the card's value and how fast you want the money.

Should I grade a card before selling it?

Only when the graded price minus the grading fee and turnaround beats what the raw card sells for today. Cheap, common cards almost never pencil out; high-end cards where a PSA 10 commands a large premium usually do. Run the raw comp and the graded comp side by side before you send anything in.

How much are eBay fees on sports cards?

Plan on roughly 13.25% plus about $0.30 per order for trading cards, applied to the full amount including shipping, before store discounts or promoted-listing costs. Always subtract fees, shipping and supplies from the sale price to see your real net before you call a flip profitable.
